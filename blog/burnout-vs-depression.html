Burnout vs Depression: How to Recognise When You Need Professional Support

Burnout and depression can share exhaustion, concentration problems and withdrawal. The safest response is not to force yourself into one label—it is to understand the limits of the comparison and choose appropriate support.

By Abraham SpringReviewed 2 September 20269 min read

Burnout vs depression: the short answer

WHO defines burnout as an occupational phenomenon linked to chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Depression is a health condition that can affect mood, interest, thinking, sleep and functioning across life. They are not interchangeable, but they can overlap. An article cannot diagnose the difference; persistent or significant symptoms deserve professional assessment.

What the definitions can—and cannot—tell you

BurnoutDepression
Formal statusWHO classifies it as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.A recognised health condition requiring clinical assessment and, where appropriate, treatment.
Core framingExhaustion, mental distance or cynicism related to work, and reduced professional effectiveness.Persistent low mood or loss of interest can occur alongside other emotional, cognitive and physical symptoms.
ContextSpecifically occupational in the WHO definition.May affect work, relationships, self-care and activities beyond one context.
AssessmentThe term should not be used to exclude other causes of exhaustion.A healthcare professional assesses symptoms, duration, impact and other possible causes.

This table is orientation, not a diagnostic tool. Real experiences rarely stay inside neat columns, and someone may experience both.

Why the overlap matters

Exhaustion, sleep changes, concentration problems, irritability, reduced motivation and withdrawal may appear in accounts of both burnout and depression. Physical illness, anxiety, medication effects, sleep disorders and substance use can also affect energy and functioning. Assuming “it is only burnout” can delay help that matters.

Equally, feeling depleted after a severe work period does not allow an article to conclude that you have depression. The responsible move is to record what is happening and seek an assessment when it persists, worsens or affects daily life.

Questions to take to a professional

  • How long have the changes been present, and are they getting worse?
  • Do they lift meaningfully away from work, or remain across most settings?
  • Have interest or pleasure changed in activities that normally matter to you?
  • How are sleep, appetite, concentration and everyday self-care affected?
  • Has medication, illness, alcohol or another substance changed?
  • Have you had thoughts of self-harm, suicide or being unable to keep yourself safe?

You do not need perfect answers before speaking to a GP. A brief record simply helps communicate the pattern.

Choose support by need, not by label

  1. GP or qualified clinician: assessment, diagnosis, medication questions, significant symptoms, risk or treatment.
  2. Therapist: appropriately qualified psychological support within their professional scope.
  3. Occupational health or employer support: workplace assessment, adjustments and work-design questions.
  4. Coach: non-clinical workload, boundaries, routines, priorities and return-to-performance planning when coaching is appropriate.

These forms of support can coexist. A good coach does not ask to become your clinician and should not make continued coaching a condition for seeking healthcare.

Abraham’s coaching boundary

My role is to help examine demands, recovery, behaviour and performance systems—not to decide whether you have depression. Where symptoms suggest that clinical care may be needed, the next responsible coaching action is referral or collaboration, not a stronger sales pitch.

What to do today

  1. Tell one trusted person what has changed.
  2. Book a GP appointment if symptoms are persistent, significant or difficult to explain.
  3. Reduce one avoidable demand while you arrange support.
  4. Do not use intense training, breathwork or meditation to override distress. Gentle practices can be optional support, not treatment.
  5. If you are in immediate danger or cannot keep yourself safe, call 999 or go to A&E. NHS 111 can direct you to urgent mental-health support.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell burnout from depression?

You cannot reliably diagnose the difference from an article. Burnout is defined by WHO as an occupational phenomenon, while depression is a health condition that can affect life more broadly. They can overlap, and significant or persistent symptoms should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

Can burnout turn into depression?

Burnout and depression are associated in research, but an individual article cannot determine cause or progression. Seek clinical assessment if low mood, loss of interest or impaired functioning persists.

Should I see a coach or therapist for burnout?

Choose clinical support when symptoms may involve depression, risk, trauma or another health condition. Coaching may complement appropriate care by addressing non-clinical workload, boundaries and routines.

Can rest tell me whether I have burnout or depression?

No. Feeling better or not feeling better after rest is useful information, but it is not a diagnostic test.
