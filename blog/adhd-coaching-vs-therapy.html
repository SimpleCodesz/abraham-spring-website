ADHD Coaching vs Therapy: Understanding the Difference

The useful question is not which profession is “better”. It is which need you are trying to meet, whether the practitioner is competent and whether the boundaries are clear.

By Abraham SpringReviewed 2 September 20269 min read

The short answer

ADHD coaching usually focuses on practical goals, systems, accountability and behaviour in the present. Therapy works within the practitioner’s professional scope with psychological, emotional or mental-health needs. Coaching is not diagnosis or treatment. Some people use both, provided the roles remain clear.

The table describes roles, not every practitioner. In the UK, terms and regulatory status vary. Verify qualifications, registration where relevant, experience and safeguarding arrangements rather than relying on a title alone.

Choose coaching when the work is practical and non-clinical

Coaching may fit when you understand the goal but struggle to translate it into a workable plan; when your routines repeatedly collapse under real-life demand; or when you need structured reflection and accountability. Examples include making priorities visible, designing a weekly rhythm, reducing context switching or integrating recovery with work and training.

A coach should still recognise limits. A practical goal does not make every underlying issue non-clinical.

Choose clinical support when assessment or treatment is needed

Clinical support should lead when you are seeking an ADHD assessment, medication advice or treatment; when distress is significant; when trauma, depression, anxiety, self-harm, substance use or another condition may be involved; or when functioning and safety are seriously affected.

NICE recommends that ADHD diagnosis is made by an appropriately qualified healthcare professional following a full clinical and psychosocial assessment. An online quiz or coach cannot provide that assessment.

When both may work together

Therapy and coaching can address different needs at the same time. The arrangement is safer when everyone understands the boundaries, confidentiality is respected and any information sharing happens with your informed agreement. You should not be asked to abandon clinical support to prove commitment to coaching.

A five-question decision guide

  1. Do I need assessment, diagnosis, medication advice or treatment? Start with an appropriately qualified healthcare professional.
  2. Is risk, trauma or significant distress present? Choose suitable clinical support.
  3. Is the main goal a practical system or accountability? Coaching may fit.
  4. Can the provider explain their limits clearly? If not, do not proceed.
  5. Would combined support be useful? Discuss roles and consent with both practitioners.

What to ask before you begin

  • What exactly is your role and relevant experience?
  • What qualifications or professional registrations do you hold?
  • How do you decide when to refer?
  • What are the confidentiality and safeguarding arrangements?
  • How will we review whether the work is helping?
  • What fees, commitments and cancellation terms apply?

Frequently asked questions

Is ADHD coaching the same as therapy?

No. Coaching is non-clinical support for practical goals and systems. Therapy is provided within the practitioner's professional scope for psychological or mental-health needs. Titles, qualifications and regulation vary, so check the individual provider.

Can I use ADHD coaching and therapy together?

They may complement each other when roles are clear, the professionals work within scope and you are comfortable with the arrangement.

Can an ADHD coach diagnose ADHD?

No. ADHD diagnosis requires assessment by an appropriately qualified healthcare professional. A coach should not confirm or rule out a diagnosis.

When should I choose clinical support first?

Choose clinical support for assessment, medication, significant mental-health symptoms, trauma, risk, severe distress or impairment that needs treatment.
