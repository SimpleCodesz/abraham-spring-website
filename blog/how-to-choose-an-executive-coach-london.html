How to Choose an Executive Coach in London

Choose for the quality of the working relationship and the clarity of the method—not for charisma, status signals or promises that cannot be measured.

By Abraham SpringReviewed 2 September 20269 min read

The short answer

Before hiring an executive coach, ask seven questions: What problem are we solving? What relevant experience do you bring? How does your method work? What sits outside your scope? How is confidentiality handled? What will progress look like? How will the relationship end? A good first conversation should make those answers clearer.

1. Define the problem before choosing the person

“Be a better leader” is too broad. Name the recurring decisions or behaviours: avoiding conflict, carrying every decision, inconsistent execution, weak boundaries or performance that relies on adrenaline. The clearer the problem, the easier it is to judge fit.

2. Ask for relevant experience—not borrowed authority

Experience can come from coaching, leadership, building organisations, performance environments or other demanding contexts. Ask how that experience relates to your situation. Do not assume a famous client list proves the coach can help you, and do not accept invented confidentiality as a substitute for explaining the work.

3. Make the method explainable

A coach should be able to describe what happens between the opening conversation and a review point. My approach uses R.A.C.E.: Recover enough capacity to see clearly; Align decisions with what matters; Condition the behaviours and systems that need repetition; Execute with feedback.

4. Check boundaries and referral judgement

Executive coaching is not psychotherapy, medical treatment, legal advice or crisis support. A responsible coach should say where their work ends and when a different professional should take priority. The International Coaching Federation's ethical framework emphasises clear agreements, confidentiality, professional conduct and accountability.

5. Understand confidentiality

If an employer sponsors the work, establish what is shared with the organisation, what remains confidential and who owns the goals. Get this clear before substantive coaching begins.

6. Agree how progress will be reviewed

Use observable evidence: decisions made sooner, fewer avoidable escalations, boundaries held, time recovered, difficult conversations completed or priorities delivered. Not everything important becomes a dashboard, but progress should be more concrete than “the sessions felt useful”.

7. Test the first conversation

Notice whether the coach listens before prescribing, can challenge without performing certainty, and leaves you with clearer ownership. Fit does not mean constant agreement. It means enough trust for honest work.

Red flags

  • Guaranteed outcomes or fixed transformation timelines.
  • Diagnosis or treatment claims outside clinical practice.
  • A method that cannot be explained.
  • Pressure to buy before scope and terms are clear.
  • Creating dependence instead of improving your judgement.
  • Vague confidentiality when a sponsor is involved.

Frequently asked questions

What should I look for in an executive coach?

Look for a clear scope, relevant experience, an explainable method, appropriate boundaries, transparent commercial terms and a way to review progress without creating dependency.

Does an executive coach need to be based in London?

Not necessarily. In-person access may matter to you, but fit, method and boundaries usually matter more. Many effective coaching relationships combine London sessions with online work.

How do I know whether executive coaching is working?

Agree observable outcomes at the start, establish a baseline and review decisions, behaviours, capacity and relevant work outcomes at defined intervals.
