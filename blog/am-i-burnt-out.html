Am I Burnt Out—or Just Tired? Signs, Context and What to Do Next

One exhausted week does not define you. But when rest stops restoring you, distance from work grows and your sense of effectiveness shrinks, the pattern deserves attention.

By Abraham SpringUpdated 2 September 202614 min read

Short answer: am I burnt out?

You may be experiencing burnout when work-related exhaustion has become persistent, rest is no longer restoring you, mental distance or cynicism is growing and you feel less effective despite continuing to push. WHO describes those three dimensions as the core occupational pattern. Burnout is not classified as a medical condition, and the same symptoms can overlap with depression, anxiety, sleep disorders and physical illness, so this article is a starting point—not a self-diagnosis.

Tiredness usually responds to recovery

Ordinary tiredness follows demand and tends to improve with adequate sleep, food, reduced load or time away. Burnout is more persistent and changes the relationship with work. You may rest without feeling restored, become detached from work you once cared about, or feel less capable despite continuing to push.

PatternWhat you may noticeWhat happens after recovery
Short-term tirednessLow energy after a demanding day, week or training block.Energy and motivation generally begin to return after adequate rest and reduced demand.
Work-related stressPressure, worry, irritability or physical tension linked to demands that feel difficult to manage.Symptoms may ease when the demand, control or support around the work improves.
Possible burnout patternPersistent exhaustion alongside growing distance or cynicism and a reduced sense of effectiveness.A short break may help temporarily, but the pattern often returns when the same conditions resume.

The categories are not a diagnostic test and they can overlap. The useful question is not simply “how tired am I?” but “what is happening to my energy, my relationship with work and my ability to recover over time?”

Common signs people notice

  • Energy depletion that is not resolving as expected.
  • Cynicism, irritability or emotional distance around work.
  • Reduced concentration, confidence or sense of effectiveness.
  • Difficulty switching off even when work has stopped.
  • Sleep disruption, headaches, muscle tension or changes in appetite.
  • Using more caffeine, alcohol, food or training intensity to manage state.

These signs are not a self-diagnosis. They are information that the current arrangement may be harming you.

Burnout can be hidden by continued performance

Output does not always collapse first. Some people continue meeting deadlines, caring for others or training consistently while the internal cost rises. They compensate with longer hours, urgency, perfectionism or more stimulation. From the outside the standard still looks high; inside, ordinary work requires more effort and recovery gives less back.

That is why performance alone is a poor test. Notice whether you are becoming less flexible: small setbacks feel larger, decisions take more energy, time away produces guilt rather than relief, and the day depends increasingly on adrenaline or avoidance. These changes do not prove burnout, but they make the pattern worth addressing before functioning deteriorates further.

A practical burnout check-in

Review the last four weeks rather than your worst day. Write down specific examples instead of scoring yourself against an online label.

  1. Energy: does ordinary rest reliably restore you, or are you starting each week already depleted?
  2. Distance: are you becoming more cynical, numb, avoidant or emotionally detached from the work?
  3. Effectiveness: does familiar work require more effort, with more mistakes or less confidence?
  4. Spillover: is the pattern affecting sleep, relationships, training, appetite or your ability to enjoy time away?
  5. Duration: is this a short response to a defined peak, or has the peak become the permanent operating model?

Take the answers seriously without treating them as a diagnosis. A record of what changed, when it changed and what makes it better or worse is also useful if you speak with a GP, therapist, occupational-health professional or coach.

Look at the work design, not only the individual

HSE identifies six areas of work design associated with stress when poorly managed: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. Review each one. A breathing practice may help tonight; it cannot resolve unclear authority, impossible workload or sustained workplace conflict on its own.

  • Demands: workload, working pattern and the physical or emotional environment.
  • Control: how much influence you have over the way the work is done.
  • Support: practical help, information and encouragement from the organisation and people around you.
  • Relationships: conflict, isolation, unacceptable behaviour or weak repair after pressure.
  • Role: whether responsibility and expectations are clear or contradictory.
  • Change: whether organisational change is communicated and managed with enough involvement and clarity.

This moves the question from “what is wrong with me?” to “which conditions are loading me, and which of them can change?” If the goal is to keep performing at a high level, read the companion guide to high performance without burnout.

What to do this week

  1. Record the pattern. Write down the symptoms, their duration and the situations in which they intensify or ease.
  2. Reduce one demand. Remove, delay, delegate or decline something non-essential rather than adding a perfect recovery routine.
  3. Create one genuine stopping point. Decide when work ends, what will remain unfinished and how tomorrow will restart.
  4. Tell someone. Speak with a person you trust instead of using continued output to hide how depleted you feel.
  5. Address the conditions. Discuss workload, control, clarity, conflict or possible adjustments where it is safe to do so.
  6. Use appropriate support. Book clinical or occupational-health help when the pattern is persistent, worsening or affecting daily functioning.

Do not force a recovery deadline. The next stage is to reduce the load maintaining the pattern and rebuild capacity gradually. The guide to burnout recovery stages explains what may change first and why there is no honest universal timeline.

Abraham’s recovery-led coaching perspective

In coaching, I use R.A.C.E.—Recover, Align, Condition, Execute—to stop recovery becoming another vague instruction to “take it easy”. Recover begins with capacity and immediate load. Align clarifies what genuinely matters and what can change. Condition rebuilds repeatability gradually. Execute returns to meaningful demand without making the old pattern the target. It is a coaching framework, not a diagnostic or treatment protocol.

The Unbroken Protocol app can provide optional structure for recovery practices and reflection. It does not diagnose burnout or replace clinical care. Breathwork and meditation are options, not requirements; if either increases distress, stop and choose a different form of support.

Where burnout coaching may fit

Burnout coaching can help you map demands, boundaries, recovery gaps and return-to-performance decisions when the work is non-clinical. It should not diagnose burnout, treat a mental-health condition or persuade you to continue through symptoms that require healthcare. When clinical and coaching support are both useful, the roles should stay clear.

When to seek clinical help

Speak with a GP if symptoms persist, worsen or interfere with daily functioning, or if you are unsure whether another condition may be involved. Seek urgent help if you feel unable to keep yourself safe. Coaching is not crisis care and should not become a delay to appropriate treatment.

Burnout is not proof that you cared too much. It is evidence that demand, conditions and recovery have stopped balancing.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if I am burnt out?

Look for a persistent work-related pattern of exhaustion, growing mental distance or cynicism, and reduced professional effectiveness. These signs cannot confirm a diagnosis and may overlap with other health conditions, so seek appropriate clinical support when symptoms are significant or persistent.

Can a weekend fix burnout?

A break may offer relief, but persistent burnout usually requires changes to the conditions producing the load as well as recovery.

How long does burnout last?

There is no reliable universal timeline. Recovery depends on the severity and duration of the pattern, the conditions maintaining it, available support and whether other health concerns are involved.

Is burnout the same as depression?

No. They can share features, and a person may experience both. A qualified healthcare professional should assess significant or persistent symptoms.

Can coaching help with burnout?

Coaching may help with workload, boundaries, recovery and return-to-performance planning. It does not diagnose or treat medical or mental-health conditions.
